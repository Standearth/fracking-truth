Natural Gas vs. Heat Pumps: The Facts, Myths, and Real Costs

Heat pumps are the perfect solution

Modern electric heat pumps keep our homes comfortable all year round — and they're cleaner, healthier, and often cheaper to run than gas.

  • Cool in the summer: Heat pumps double as air conditioners, keeping families safe during heat waves
  • Clean air on smoky days: Because heat pumps cool your home, you can keep the windows shut on hot, smoky wildfire days — and with a good filter they help clean indoor air. Unlike gas appliances, they don't create indoor air pollution either
  • Warm in the winter: Today's cold-climate heat pumps heat homes efficiently, even in the coldest temperatures
  • Lower bills: Heat pumps use about ⅓ of the energy of a gas furnace, so many B.C. households can save on their energy bills
  • Good for the climate: Running on B.C.'s mostly clean electricity, heat pumps dramatically cut the carbon pollution from heating our homes

Electric heat pumps

Heat pumps are a win-win-win for the climate, our wallets, and public health.

  • Heat pumps double as air conditioners in the summer, ensuring proper cooling in hot summer weather and making homes much safer for vulnerable residents during extreme heat events
  • With the addition of a filter, heat pumps can provide air purification which is critical as wildfire smoke affects more people. Because they cool homes, heat pumps also let residents keep windows shut on hot, smoky days
  • Modern electric heat pumps are incredibly efficient appliances that allow us to heat and cool our homes with far less energy than gas heating systems
  • The average heat pump uses about ⅓ of the energy of a gas furnace and uses electricity as an input, resulting in lower greenhouse gas emissions - by 45% to 85%
  • Because heat pumps are highly efficient and use electricity as a power source, they lead to lower energy bills for B.C. residents. A 2025 Clean Energy Canada study found the average B.C. household switching to a cold-climate heat pump would save $169 a year compared to a gas furnace with air conditioning — and $849 a year compared to electric baseboards with air conditioning
  • Heat pumps also offer stable monthly energy costs because electricity is often provided by crown corporations that are mandated to keep electricity prices low. This in contrast to volatile global gas prices that are impacted by global events
  • Advancements in heat pump technology mean they can now heat homes in the coldest of temperatures, including in northern regions of Canada
  • Many places are embracing heat pumps including European countries and the United States
  • Modern electric heat pumps are several times more efficient than old school electric baseboard heaters which means they are less costly to operate
  • Equipping homes with heat pumps from the outset of construction means avoiding the cost and hassle of retrofits in the future
  • Unlike gas equipment, heat pumps don’t emit poisonous chemicals, and replacing gas stoves with electric stoves greatly reduces asthma symptoms
  • Building electrification could be a significant job creator, and the global energy transition is a major economic opportunity for Metro Vancouver
  • Heat pumps are a perfect heating and cooling solution but there are other low-carbon heating options for large new builds, including geothermal and district energy

  • While heat pumps can cost more upfront than gas furnaces, they provide both heating and cooling. Gas systems, by contrast, rely on gas lines that are expensive to build and maintain — costs ultimately borne by residents
  • Installing heat pumps at the construction stage can speed up construction timelines since there is no need to install pipelines or gas hookups
  • Building electric homes has been shown to cost a similar amount or even less than gas-reliant homes. See sources: Source 1, Source 2, Source 3, Source 4, Source 5
  • Green buildings offer long-term operational savings allowing residents to save money on energy costs
  • Many developers across the country have been building green homes for decades - and say they are cheaper and superior to gas-dependent homes
  • Buildings constructed today will likely still be standing in 2050, so it’s better to build them right the first time instead of saddling future residents with costly retrofits
  • In a typical Lower Mainland single-family home, a cold-climate heat pump costs about $1,500 to $4,500 more upfront than a gas furnace plus central air conditioning, before rebates — a gap that is paid back through lower bills, and that shrinks further when homes avoid paying for a gas connection

  • As we transition all energy to renewables, even if currently electricity is partly generated with fracked gas, heat pumps are more efficient so they lead to lower fossil fuel use and emissions than directly using natural gas or oil to heat buildings.
  • Clean energy is already the main source of electricity in Canada, where about 80% of electricity comes from non-emitting sources, and the supply of wind and solar is growing quickly
  • Communities have been building local renewable power generation and more and more homes are using solar panels and battery storage, leading the way to cleaner and more local power generation
  • The federal Clean Electricity Regulations remain law and keep the goal of a net-zero grid by 2050. In 2026 the federal government paused them in Alberta and proposed more flexibility for gas power plants elsewhere. B.C.'s grid is already mostly clean hydro, so heat pumps here run on low-carbon power regardless
  • Globally, electricity generated by renewables is getting cheaper and new storage and grid developments will enable emissions-free energy electricity
  • Most gas furnaces have parts that run on electricity so the appliance doesn’t work when the power goes out
  • Some people use gas generators or gas fireplaces as a backup heat source but these systems come with the risks and health impacts of gas and can be expensive
  • The best protection from blackouts is moving to high-efficiency heating and improving grid reliability - not expensive, polluting backups for each individual building
  • Battery backups like using electric vehicles (EVs) to power homes are good backup solutions for heat pumps that are becoming increasingly available and affordable
  • Because we are inevitably moving towards electrification, electric utilities and transmission authorities are already planning for increased electricity generation and transmission to meet growing needs
  • Interest in electrification is speeding up the process to meet increased energy demands
  • Modeling has found that Canada can meet its needs for clean electricity “reliably and affordably” through a combination of growing solar and wind power generation, new transmission connections between provinces, and other grid improvements
  • Municipalities are also preparing for increased electricity needs for buildings, and as green buildings become the norm, it will become easier to secure additional energy for new builds.
  • Extra power can easily be generated with clean sources such as wind and solar (along with existing storage technology), ensuring that the electricity sector doesn’t become more polluting itself.
  • British Columbia: BC Hydro is building new clean power to keep up with growing demand. A historic drought starting in 2022 meant B.C. had to import more power than usual — but imports have dropped sharply as water levels recovered and Site C came fully online in 2025. BC Hydro's 2024 and 2025 calls for power will add about 8,500 GWh a year of new wind and solar by 2033 — much of it with First Nations ownership — backed by a $36 billion, 10-year capital plan. Its Integrated Resource Plan plans for growth from both industry and electrification. And because heat pumps are so efficient, switching every B.C. home to a heat pump would actually reduce the electricity used for heating and cooling by about 800 GWh a year — even after adding cooling to homes that don't have it today
  • British Columbia — FortisBC: Some B.C. communities, including much of Kelowna and parts of the South Okanagan and Kootenays, get their electricity from FortisBC rather than BC Hydro, while Penticton and Summerland run their own municipal utilities. FortisBC generates hydro power from its own plants on the Kootenay River and buys the rest from BC Hydro and other producers. Its electricity rates are somewhat higher than BC Hydro's, but heat pumps are still the most efficient way to heat and cool a home in these communities

Questions we hear at council

Answers to the most common concerns raised when local governments consider the Zero Carbon Step Code for new buildings.

  • If most builders already build all-electric homes, a bylaw costs them little or nothing — it simply makes it the standard for everyone
  • A clear rule creates a level playing field, so builders who do the right thing aren’t undercut, and homebuyers know every new home meets the same standard
  • The Zero Carbon Step Code is a provincial tool that gives builders a predictable, consistent standard across B.C. communities
  • Building standards are about protecting the people who live in a home — just like fire sprinklers. With extreme heat becoming more common, building new homes with heat pumps means they can keep residents cool and safe

  • Builders don’t pay the energy bills — the people who live in the home do. Without a standard, the cheapest option for the builder isn’t always the cheapest option for the homeowner or renter over the life of the building
  • Homes built today will still be standing in 2050. Building them right the first time avoids saddling future residents with costly retrofits

  • The Zero Carbon Step Code is a performance standard for new buildings — it sets a limit on carbon pollution, not a list of banned appliances
  • Homeowners still choose their heating and cooling system, brand, and design; the standard simply makes sure new homes are built for the future
  • Without a standard, the choice is made by developers, not by the people who will live in and pay to heat the home

  • The Zero Carbon Step Code only applies to new construction — existing homes, and the gas appliances in them, aren’t affected
  • In many municipalities, decorative fireplaces and gas barbecues are not counted towards compliance for houses

  • Heat pumps are so efficient that switching every B.C. home to a heat pump would reduce the electricity used for heating and cooling by about 800 GWh a year
  • BC Hydro is adding about 8,500 GWh a year of new wind and solar power through its 2024 and 2025 calls for power, along with new capacity at existing dams like Revelstoke
  • Utilities plan years ahead for growth, and clear local building standards help them plan better

  • No. Existing buildings and retrofits are not impacted by the Zero Carbon Step Code — it applies only to new construction
  • No one is required to replace a working furnace — homeowners can switch to a heat pump on their own timeline, for example when their furnace or air conditioner needs replacing

  • Provincial analysis found that building to the highest Zero Carbon Step Code level adds 1% or less to the construction cost of houses in Victoria’s climate zone
  • Housing affordability includes the monthly cost of living there. In Kelowna, a cold-climate heat pump saves a typical household around $110 to $200 a year compared to gas plus air conditioning — and about half of B.C. households don’t have air conditioning at all (Clean Energy Canada, 2025)
  • Building all-electric avoids the cost of gas hookups and future retrofits

  • Local governments don’t control energy exports — but they do decide how homes in their community are built, and that decision affects local families for decades
  • Locking new homes into gas means residents pay for gas connections and volatile gas prices for the life of the building, regardless of what happens with LNG
  • The future is electric. As more homes go electric, the cost of maintaining the gas system is spread over fewer customers — so building new homes on gas leaves future residents exposed to rising delivery costs

Gas is the worst

Gas is marketed as “natural” and “green” but it’s neither of those - and has many problems.

  • Most gas is extracted through hydraulic fracturing, also known as fracking, where water and toxic chemicals are forced deep into the ground to release the fossil fuel
  • Fracking uses a lot of water and leads to dangerous groundwater contamination
  • Fracking impacts both rural and Indigenous communities in B.C. and has been linked to health problems (including birth defects, asthma, and cancer) in neighbouring communities
  • In particular, Indigenous communities located near fracking sites experience negative health outcomes resulting from the release of toxic chemicals
  • Gas leaks are common along transportation routes and can lead to explosions, causing injuries
  • Racialized and low-income communities bear disproportionate health and safety impacts from gas production and use

  • Fracked gas is mainly comprised of the greenhouse gas methane, which has 84 to 86 times more of an impact on global warming than carbon dioxide over a 20-year period
  • Methane is responsible for around 30% of the current rise in global temperature since the Industrial Revolution
  • When fracked gas is purposely flared at wells and when it leaks during extraction and transportation, methane escapes
  • In Canada, buildings — mostly from burning fracked gas for heat — are one of the largest sources of emissions nationally, accounting for 12 percent of the country’s total pollution
  • In municipalities building emissions are often a top polluter

  • Fracked gas is highly polluting and leaks at fracking sites, in aging pipe infrastructure, and also in our own homes
  • Burning fossil gas produces indoor air pollution and contributes to smog
  • Relying on gas furnaces means housing is connected to gas lines, which often leads to the use of gas appliances like gas stoves – which have been linked to asthma in children and adults. A 2026 study found that replacing gas stoves with electric stoves improved asthma symptoms and cut asthma-related emergency room visits and hospitalizations by 70%

Dangerous Distractions

The Gas industry is peddling false solutions like renewable natural gas (RNG), liquified natural gas (LNG), and hydrogen - to keep selling us more fracked gas.

  • “Renewable” natural gas (RNG) is gas produced by sources such as wastewater, landfills, and agricultural waste
  • This product is very expensive - around 4 to 17 times more expensive than regular gas
  • There is nowhere near enough RNG to replace conventional gas
  • If it’s going to be used at all, it should be in hard to decarbonize sectors like heavy industry
  • Using RNG for building heating is a waste of resources since this function can easily be accomplished with clean electricity
  • Buying into RNG infrastructure means the continued construction of gas infrastructure through the building of pipelines and facilities – unintentionally incentivizing the industry to continue business as usual
  • RNG has most of the same negative impacts as regular gas, including poor indoor air quality and safety
  • Just like fracked gas, RNG is almost entirely methane, and would be transmitted through the same system of leaky pipes and combustion units

  • Liquified “Natural” Gas (LNG) is created by cooling fracked gas to -160ºC, creating a liquid that is 600 times smaller than fracked gas
  • LNG is just fracked gas which has been cooled down dramatically to make it easier for transport across distances – but it is a fossil fuel like any other
  • The carbon footprint of LNG has been shown to be worse than that of coal

  • Most hydrogen is made using fracked gas, so producing and using it results in significant amounts of greenhouse gases
  • There is a limited amount of what’s called “green hydrogen,” which is hydrogen created using renewable energy
  • Green hydrogen is very expensive and not as effective or efficient as traditional renewable energy
  • It’s not safe to use any kind of hydrogen at concentrations over a few percent in much of the piping we use for fracked gas because it can create holes in the pipes that both methane and hydrogen will leak through
  • It’s more efficient to use renewable energy to power homes directly than to make expensive “green hydrogen”
  • Replacing all the pipes and appliances we use to accommodate hydrogen use would be extraordinarily expensive and unnecessary compared to electrification
